Part 1: Masses and springs

The cloth is modeled as a grid of point masses joined by springs. Cloth::buildGrid builds this grid in two passes: first all of the point masses, then all of the springs.

Point masses

For a cloth with num_width_points \(\times\) num_height_points masses, the masses are evenly spaced so that mass \((0, 0)\) sits at the origin of the cloth's plane and the last mass sits at \((\text{width}, \text{height})\). The spacing is therefore \[ \Delta x = \frac{\text{width}}{\text{num_width_points} - 1}, \qquad \Delta y = \frac{\text{height}}{\text{num_height_points} - 1}. \] The two orientations differ in how the grid is placed:

  • Horizontal: the cloth lies flat in the \(xz\)-plane at height \(y = 1\). The mass at grid index \((i, j)\) is placed at \((i\,\Delta x,\ 1,\ j\,\Delta y)\).
  • Vertical: the cloth hangs in the \(xy\)-plane at \((i\,\Delta x,\ j\,\Delta y,\ z)\), where \(z\) is a small random offset in \([-10^{-3}, 10^{-3}]\). The offset keeps the cloth from being perfectly planar, so it does not stay unnaturally flat once it starts moving.

A mass is marked as pinned if its \((i, j)\) grid index appears in the scene's pinned list. The masses are stored in row-major order: mass \((i, j)\) is at index \(j \cdot \text{num_width_points} + i\) of the 1D point_masses vector. The cloth mesh used for rendering assumes this layout.

Springs

The springs are added only after every mass exists. Each spring stores raw pointers to its two masses, so the mass vector must not reallocate while springs are being added. The vector's capacity is reserved up front to guarantee that. Each mass is connected only to neighbors to its left or above it. Every pair of masses is therefore visited once, so no spring is created twice.

  • Structural: to the mass directly left of it and the mass directly above it. These springs resist stretching and compression along the weave of the cloth.
  • Shearing: to the masses diagonally upper-left and upper-right of it. These springs resist the cloth's squares skewing into parallelograms (in-plane shear).
  • Bending: to the mass two to the left and the mass two above it. These springs resist the cloth folding sharply over a single row or column of masses.

At the edges of the grid, a spring is added only if its neighbor actually exists, so no spring reaches outside the grid. For the \(50 \times 50\) grid in pinned2.json, this gives \(4900\) structural, \(4802\) shearing, and \(4800\) bending springs, for \(14{,}502\) springs in total.

Raising density makes each point mass heavier, so the same spring force holds it up less and the cloth stretches more. With no damping, the cloth never loses energy and never comes to rest. Part 3: Handling collisions with other objects

After each Verlet step, Cloth::simulate calls collide on every collision object for every point mass. Both collide methods find a corrected target point \(p\) and then set \[ \text{position} = \text{last_position} + (1 - f)\,(p - \text{last_position}), \] where \(f\) is the object's friction. With \(f = 0\), the mass moves all the way to \(p\). With larger \(f\), it moves less, which slows it as it slides along the surface.

  • Sphere: a mass collides if its distance to the center is at most the radius \(r\). Then \(p\) is the point on the surface in the direction of the mass: \(p = c + r\,\frac{x - c}{\|x - c\|}\), where \(c\) is the center and \(x\) is position.
  • Plane: a mass collides if position and last_position are on opposite sides of the plane. The sign of \((x - q) \cdot n\) gives the side, where \(q\) is a point on the plane and \(n\) is its normal. Then \(p\) is position projected onto the plane, moved SURFACE_OFFSET back toward the side the mass came from.

Part 4: Handling self-collisions

Checking every pair of point masses would take \(O(n^2)\) time, so I use a spatial hash instead. Each step, Cloth::simulate rebuilds the hash and then calls self_collide on every point mass.

  • hash_position splits space into boxes of size \(w \times h \times t\), where \(w = 3 \cdot \text{width} / \text{num_width_points}\), \(h = 3 \cdot \text{height} / \text{num_height_points}\), and \(t = \max(w, h)\). It finds the integer box index \((i, j, k)\) of a position on each axis and returns \(128^2 i + 128 j + k\) as the key.
  • build_spatial_map adds every point mass to the list for its key.
  • self_collide looks at the other masses in the same box. For each one closer than \(2 \cdot \text{thickness}\), it computes the correction that would push this mass directly away from it until they are exactly \(2 \cdot \text{thickness}\) apart. It averages these corrections, divides by simulation_steps, and moves only this mass. Pinned masses are skipped.
